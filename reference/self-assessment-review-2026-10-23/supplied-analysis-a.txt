**Objective analysis of the parallels and practical advice on developing the HDRL self-assessment and agentic assessment.**

### What each offers

**HDRL Framework** (Health Data Readiness Level) is an open, evidence-informed maturity framework for organisational and system readiness for trusted, federated health data research. It has 8 domains, 64 indicators, and 5 maturity levels (L1 Initial → L5 Optimising). It was developed from a synthesis of 56 source frameworks, first applied formatively in the 3 Nations Readiness Assessment (Scotland, Wales, Northern Ireland), and published under CC BY 4.0. It is explicitly *not* an accreditation or programme participation standard. Its core value is making the “missing middle” visible: bridging high-level principles (Five Safes, public benefit) and detailed technical specifications, while distinguishing capability gaps, evidence gaps, and capacity gaps. Assessment is scoped (system / service / dual), evidence-first, and produces improvement roadmaps. You already have strong machine-readable assets (JSON indicator catalogue with L1–L5 descriptors and minimum evidence requirements, JSON Schema, Markdown source of record, `llms.txt`).

**SmarterX AI Score** is a free companion platform to *The AI Transformation Blueprint*. It offers two quick self-assessments: Business AI Score (8 pillars, 67 statements, 12–15 minutes) and Personal AI Score (5 pillars, 37 statements, 8–10 minutes). Users rate plain-language statements on a 1–5 scale or “Not Sure” (which counts as zero and is treated as a real gap). Outputs are an overall percentage, pillar grades (Weak 0–59% / Moderate 60–79% / Strong 80–100%), a visual pillar map, strongest/focus areas, and direct links to action ladders in the Blueprint. It supports team reports (perception gaps), progress tracking, and privacy-by-design. It is positioned as a leadership/change-management tool, not a technical audit. The CEO prototyped it extensively with Claude before handing it to a development team.

### Clear parallels and important differences

**Parallels**
- Structured multi-domain/pillar maturity view that produces both an overall picture and granular priorities.
- Explicit aim to turn assessment into actionable next steps (roadmaps vs action ladders).
- Support for organisational and more individual/local views.
- Emphasis on honest current-state assessment rather than aspirational scoring.
- Open methodology and potential to feed longer-term improvement or education offerings.
- Both already recognise that “Not Sure” / missing evidence is informative rather than neutral.

**Differences that matter for your design**
- AI Score is optimised for speed, low friction, and pure self-rating. HDRL’s intellectual core is the separation of assertion from evidence and the formal maturity descriptors with minimum evidence requirements. Diluting that would weaken the framework’s distinct contribution in a high-stakes domain.
- AI Score is tightly coupled to a proprietary Blueprint and education product. HDRL is an open framework with existing public machine-readable artefacts—this is a significant advantage for agentic tooling.
- HDRL already handles units of assessment (system/service/dual), inheritance rules, foundational indicators, and applicability classes. AI Score is simpler.
- AI Score treats the assessment as a leadership diagnostic that can be completed in minutes with no preparation. A full HDRL assessment is currently more demanding by design.

### How to develop the self-assessment and agentic assessment most effectively

Your existing assets (especially the JSON catalogue with descriptors + minimum evidence requirements) put you in a stronger starting position for agentic work than most maturity frameworks.

**1. Offer two complementary modes rather than one compromise**

- **Light / awareness self-assessment** (closer to AI Score): plain-language statements mapped to indicators or domain clusters. 15–25 minutes. Produces readiness profile (Emerging / Developing / Managed / Optimising), domain visual, focus areas, and “evidence you would need to raise confidence.” Treat “Not Sure” or “we think so but have no documented evidence” as signals of evidence gaps. This is the entry point.
- **Evidence-backed or agent-assisted assessment**: uses the full descriptors. Users (or an agent) supply or link evidence; the system proposes levels with rationale, confidence, and explicit capability/evidence/capacity gap tagging. Human calibration and Right-of-Reply equivalent remain central. This preserves HDRL’s methodological integrity.

Do not force the rigorous version to feel like a 67-statement Likert survey; the value is in the evidence distinction.

**2. Design the agentic layer around the existing catalogue**

The JSON already contains what agents need: indicator ID, domain, type/class/unit, L1–L5 descriptors, and minimum evidence requirements for higher levels. Practical agent patterns that fit well:

- Evidence ingestion agent: takes uploaded policies, metrics, public reports, or (with permission) repository links → maps fragments to indicators → extracts candidate evidence with citations.
- Scoring agent: proposes a maturity level against the official descriptors, states the supporting evidence (or lack of it), flags uncertainty, and distinguishes gap types. Require structured output that cites the specific descriptor language.
- Consistency / calibration agent: checks scoring patterns across related indicators and against foundational indicator logic.
- Roadmap agent: turns the gap pattern into a sequenced set of improvement actions, respecting the user’s stated scope and constraints.

Keep a human-in-the-loop for final judgements, especially on foundational indicators and any external-facing summaries. Because the source of record is versioned and checksummed, agents can be instructed to use only the published v1.0.1 descriptors.

**3. Borrow the strongest UX and engagement lessons from AI Score**

- Immediate, visual outputs (domain radar or bar chart, readiness profile, clear focus areas) rather than a long table of levels.
- Explicit “what this means / what to do next” language tied back to the framework.
- Progress saving, ability to pause, and retake/tracking over time.
- Multi-stakeholder or team view that surfaces perception differences without ranking individuals.
- Low-friction start (no account required for a first pass; optional save/account for tracking and team features).
- Plain-language framing with optional deeper “why this matters” or descriptor access.

**4. Positioning and commercial path**

AI Score is free and acts as a companion that drives people toward the Blueprint and AI Academy. A similar logic can work here: free or low-friction self-assessment as the public face of the open framework, with facilitated assessments, agentic tooling subscriptions, training, or advisory as the paid layer. Emphasise that HDRL complements (does not replace) SATRE, Five Safes, ISO 27001, etc.

Because the framework is already open and has a public machine-readable catalogue, you can also encourage third-party tools while maintaining a reference implementation.

**5. Practical development sequence**

1. Map a reduced set of plain-language statements to the indicators (or to domain clusters first) and test completion rates and perceived usefulness.
2. Build a simple agent prototype that takes the JSON catalogue + a set of documents and produces a draft scoring sheet with rationale and gap types. Validate against the original 3 Nations evidence or new pilot cases.
3. Design the output artefacts (readiness profile, gap table distinguishing capability/evidence/capacity, prioritised roadmap) so they are useful even before full automation.
4. Add team/multi-perspective views and progress tracking once the core scoring logic is stable.
5. Keep versioning and checksum discipline so agents and tools always reference a specific published framework version.

### Risks to manage

- Over-simplifying into pure self-rating would lose the evidence-based differentiator that makes HDRL valuable in health data research.
- In a high-trust domain, clear disclaimers (not accreditation, scores reflect available evidence, further independent validation still required) remain important.
- Agentic scoring can hallucinate evidence or over-confidently match descriptors; force citation of source text and confidence/uncertainty flags.
- Scope definition (system vs service) and applicability rules must stay explicit, or comparisons become misleading.

The strongest path is to treat the light self-assessment as the accessible front door (learning from AI Score’s speed and action orientation) while using the existing machine-readable descriptors and evidence requirements as the foundation for a higher-integrity agent-assisted assessment that still requires human calibration. That combination leverages what you have already built and what AI Score has demonstrated works for engagement, without collapsing the methodological differences that give HDRL its specific value.
